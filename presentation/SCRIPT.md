# Pitch video: narration script

Spoken by the ElevenLabs AI voice "Jarnathan".

Can your computing power heat a home while doing useful work? We're DigitalHeat, and we put servers in the basements of older, poorly insulated houses in Basel that still heat with oil or gas. Our servers replace that boiler, with an existing wood stove as backup for the coldest weather. In an oil-heated reference home, that saves about three thousand seven hundred litres of heating oil a year. In the case model, eight servers cover ninety-three percent of a reference home's heat. Over a year, they dump just five kilowatt-hours, run at fifty-two percent utilisation, and finish every urgent job on time. On energy alone, they break even at twenty-three Swiss centimes per server-hour. That assumes unlimited docking work, and zero power while paused. When we include idle power and switch servers off, they still cover ninety point five percent.

So how does it work? Our model connects twenty homes and a public sports pool through one shared job queue. Our main job is molecular docking for pharma research. It can pause and, in our model, has no deadline. Urgent microscopy analysis can't pause and is due within two hours. Every hour, the scheduler first runs whatever must start now to meet its deadline. Then it adds docking work, until it has covered the heat demand and the free space in the tank. Everything else waits. And our heat router decides where each job runs: homes come first, and the pool takes what they can't use.

Let's go into one of those homes on the fifteenth of January. It's below minus five degrees outside, so all eight servers compute around the clock. We assume eighty percent of their power is recovered as heat in the water. That covers about seventy percent of the day's demand, and the existing wood stove supplies the rest.

Now it's the seventh of March, and this job is real. It's molecular docking on our laptop, which plays server eight. The house and heat are simulated. The live ticker counts every result, and the process is controlled by the same scheduler code that calculates our yearly balance. Now watch the tank. As the day warms up, the house needs less heat, so the tank keeps charging. At two p.m., it reaches its limit of thirty-five kilowatt-hours, and the scheduler immediately freezes the real process. The counter stops, but the job keeps all its progress. Then, as the evening cools and the house needs more heat, the very same process carries on.

But pausing isn't our only option, because work can also move. Here's a day in late May, with replayed docking scores and simulated heat. The screening starts in the house. When the tank is full, the job saves a checkpoint and hands it over, encrypted, to the pool: a second process on the same laptop. It picks up at molecule nine.

That flexibility matters most in summer. On the fifteenth of July, the home needs only twelve kilowatt-hours, for hot water. One server is enough, and seven stand by. The router sends docking work to the Saint Jakob sports pool, where fifty modelled servers help heat the water. But this needs enough jobs. With limited demand, our fleet loses money, even with the pool.

To plan ahead, we trained a machine learning model to forecast our modelled heat demand up to six hours ahead. It uses gradient boosting, and learns from weather forecasts, time, season, and past demand. On months it had never seen, its error was about ten percent lower than simply copying yesterday. But in our planning experiment, it saved almost no extra energy, so it doesn't steer the live router yet. The big lever is switching idle servers off. For customers, what counts is predictability. So at noon, we take the weather forecast, run a copy of our scheduler through tomorrow, and keep a safety margin. That lets the fleet commit about ninety-one percent of its server-hours a day ahead.

So, does it pay off? Equipping one home costs about two hundred and ninety thousand Swiss francs, and the household gets the heat for free. If every hour sells at our target prices, DigitalHeat earns about thirty-five thousand Swiss francs per home a year, after costs, tax and interest. With interest included, the investment pays for itself in three point three years. After five years, we're about one hundred and ninety thousand Swiss francs ahead, including server resale. With only an anchor customer, we lose six thousand four hundred a year. We need to sell at least sixty-six percent of the hours. Heat isn't the bottleneck, paying demand is, and no customer has confirmed our prices yet.

That's why we're open about what you've just seen. The docking process, the pause, and the checkpoint transfer are real. The scores and routing in that handover are replayed. Homes, servers, heat and revenue are simulated, on real Basel weather.

Our next step is a pilot: one home, two servers, one winter, for eighty-one thousand five hundred Swiss francs. We'll measure sales, prices, hardware costs, idle power, and the reliability of our commitments, each against a threshold we've set in advance. If they all hold, we scale to twenty homes. If not, we stop or adapt, and move or resell the servers.

The world will keep on computing. We just think it should happen where someone needs the heat. DigitalHeat: useful computing, useful heat.
